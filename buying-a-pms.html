What operators get wrong when buying a PMS

A property management system is a ten-year decision made on the strength of a ninety-minute demo, usually by a committee measuring the wrong things.

Selection processes tend to converge on a feature matrix. Every vendor gets a column, every requirement gets a row, and whoever accumulates the most ticks wins. It feels rigorous. It mostly measures which vendor is best at filling in spreadsheets.

The trouble is that features are the most visible and least durable part of a platform. Anything missing today might ship next year. The things that won't change are the ones nobody scored.

What you're actually buying

The data model. How the system thinks about a booking, a guest, a rate, a night, a modification. You will be living inside those assumptions for a decade, and no configuration screen will let you out. If it can't represent the way you actually sell — long stays, sites versus rooms, groups, annuals, whatever your business really does — the gap never closes. It just becomes a workaround someone maintains forever.

Data access. Can you get your own data out, in bulk, on your own schedule, without asking? A vendor whose answer is "we can build you a report" has told you something important about the next ten years. This one question predicts more future pain than any feature on the matrix.

The integration posture. Not whether an API exists, but whether it's documented, versioned, and treated as a product. Ask when it last had a breaking change and how much notice customers got.

The release cadence. Frequent updates mean progress and disruption. Rare updates mean stability and stagnation. Neither is wrong — but find out which one you're signing up for, because it determines how much of your year gets spent on regression testing.

Features are what you see in the demo. The data model is what you argue with at two in the morning three years later.

The exit is part of the purchase

Almost nobody negotiates the exit while they still have leverage, which is exactly when they have it. Before signing, get answers in writing: what format is my data returned in, how long does it take, what does it cost, and does it include history rather than just current-state records.

A migration where you can only extract live bookings and not five years of history isn't a migration. It's starting again with a spreadsheet of the past.

A better shortlist process

  • Bring your own scenarios. Don't watch their demo — hand them five things you genuinely do, including the awkward ones, and make them show you.
  • Sit with the people who'll use it. Twenty minutes of a front desk trying a real check-in beats any amount of executive walkthrough.
  • Talk to a reference the vendor didn't choose. Find an operator your size who left, and ask why.
  • Count the clicks. The most common task, done a hundred times a day, is your real cost of ownership.
  • Price the whole stack. Licence, implementation, integrations, training, the interfaces you'll need in year two, and the internal time nobody puts in the business case.

And the honest part

There is no perfect system. Every option involves accepting a set of compromises, and the right question isn't which platform is best — it's which set of compromises you can live with for a decade, given how your business actually operates.

Teams that pick well tend to be the ones who worked out what they truly couldn't compromise on before anyone booked a demo. Teams that pick badly tend to be the ones who let a very good salesperson define the criteria for them.
