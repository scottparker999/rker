The lowest rate worth taking.

Linen, amenities and the clean are consumed per stay. Energy and water are consumed per night. So a room doesn't have a cost — it has a curve, and your floor moves with length of stay.

The property

Fixed costs are everything that doesn't change when you sell one more room — management, public areas, insurance, rates, base energy, maintenance. They matter for the cost per occupied room and not at all for the floor.

Cleaning

The departure clean lands once per stay; the stayover service lands on the nights in between. Use a fully loaded hourly rate — base, on-costs and the productivity allowance all in — or work it out properly in the housekeeping model.

Linen

Laundry is roughly half of what linen costs. The rest is wear — a set has a finite number of washes in it, and every changeover spends a slice of that — and loss to stains, tears and suitcases. Leave those out and your replacement budget is a surprise every year instead of a line item.

Consumables and utilities

Energy here is the marginal draw of a sold room, not the meter divided by room nights — the base load runs whether or not anybody is in it, and belongs in fixed costs above.

Method

Cost per occupied room is an allocation, not a cost. Write it out and the shape is obvious: it equals the per-night cost, plus the per-stay cost divided by average length of stay, plus fixed cost divided by occupied room nights. Only that last term moves with occupancy — and it moves because you divided by a bigger number, not because anything got cheaper. Filling the house does not reduce your costs. It spreads them.

So the floor is a curve, not a number. Linen, amenities and the departure clean are consumed once per stay; energy, water and stayover servicing are consumed every night. A one-nighter carries a whole changeover on a single night's rate. A week-long stay spreads it over seven. Two bookings at the same nightly rate are not the same business.

There are two honest floors. Tonight, with the roster published and paid, cleaning labour is already spent — so the floor for a room that would otherwise go out empty is just what the room burns: power, water, consumables and the linen it turns. Over a season, staffing follows volume, labour is genuinely variable, and it belongs in the floor. Use tonight's number for a distressed room and the season number for a rate you publish, and never the other way round. The step behaviour behind the short-run figure is modelled in the housekeeping model.

Linen is costed at wear and loss, not just laundry. A set has a finite number of washes in it, so every changeover consumes a slice of the purchase price, and a proportion never comes back at all. On the defaults here that's a third of the true cost, sitting outside the laundry invoice where nobody sees it.

Energy is the marginal draw only. Dividing the electricity bill by room nights buries the base load — reception, corridors, plant, the pool — in a per-room figure and makes an empty night look expensive. Base load belongs in fixed costs.

This pairs with the OTA vs direct calculator: that one tells you what a booking nets after the channel takes its cut, this one tells you what it costs to honour. A booking is worth taking when the first number beats the second, which is a different test from beating your ADR.

What this does not model: rate elasticity, so nothing here says what you could charge; the opportunity cost of selling cheap early and displacing a better booking later, which is the displacement calculator; wear on the room itself and the refurbishment cycle it pulls forward; credit card fees and commission, which sit on the revenue side; and the reputational cost of a rate your regulars find on a discount channel.

Everything here runs in your browser. No figures are sent anywhere, nothing is stored, and there is no analytics on this page.
