What a late checkout really costs.

Most of a housekeeping day is committed the moment the roster is published. This works out what is genuinely fixed, what is variable, and how many late checkouts you can hand out before one of them costs you a shift.

Roster and pay

Nobody cleans for eight straight hours. The productivity allowance covers breaks, briefing and walking between units — rostering as though it doesn't exist guarantees a shortfall by mid-morning.

Late checkout

A late checkout doesn't create extra work — it's the same clean, pushed into a narrower window. So it costs nothing while the rostered shift can still absorb it, and a real amount the moment it can't. Against that sits whatever the guest spends by still being on the property at lunchtime.

Length of stay

Average length of stay gets argued as a revenue metric. It is also a cost lever: for the same room nights sold, a longer stay is one fewer departure clean and a few more cheap stayover services. This prices that side of it.

Method

Most of a housekeeping day is fixed before it starts. Shifts are committed in advance, and once they are, the marginal cost of one more clean inside them is nothing — you have already bought the hours. The cost only appears when the work crosses a shift boundary, and then it arrives all at once. Housekeeping is a step function, not a slope, which is why asking "what does a clean cost" gives you an average that is wrong in both directions.

A late checkout is the same work in a narrower window. It does not add minutes; it delays them. So it is free while the rostered shift can still take it, and costs real overtime the moment it cannot — and that threshold moves every day with the departure count and the staffing. On a day the normal work already runs past the late checkout time there is nothing left to give; on a quiet day there is a surprising amount.

Stayovers are derived, never entered. Rooms occupied tonight less arrivals today is definitionally who stayed through. Where consecutive days don't reconcile — last night's occupancy should equal today's departures plus tonight's stayovers — the tool says so rather than quietly picking one.

Integer rostering is modelled deliberately. You cannot roster three tenths of a person, so required hours round up to whole shifts and the difference is reported as slack. That slack is real money, it recurs every week, and it is invisible in any model that works in fractional hours.

Weekend loading comes from the date. Penalty rates are a genuine driver of what a Saturday costs, and a departure-heavy Saturday compounds the two. Public holidays get a per-day override.

Longer stays are not automatically cheaper. Raising average stay removes a departure clean but adds the stayover servicing that replaces it, so it only saves money while a departure clean costs more than the servicing of the nights it makes way for. Service daily and thoroughly with a quick turnover and it inverts — longer stays cost more. The tool works out which side of that line you are on rather than assuming, and says so.

The argument behind this — why the cheapest thing on your rate card is the one you give away without pricing — is in The cost of a checkout.

What this does not model: deep cleans and periodic programmes; linen and consumables, which are a real cost but not a labour one; maintenance and turndown; supervision, inspection and training overhead; the quality cost of rushing a clean to hit a departure board; and room-readiness timing against arrival time, which is a sequencing problem rather than a volume one. On the late checkout side it assumes the room is not needed for an arriving guest — on a sold-out night that is the binding constraint rather than labour, and it is a different calculation. Nor does it model the goodwill of saying yes, which is real and unquantifiable. In-house against contract cleaning is a genuine decision with a genuine threshold, and it deserves its own tool rather than a mode bolted onto this one.
