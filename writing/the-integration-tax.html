The integration tax

Every vendor in this industry says they integrate. Almost all of them are telling the truth, and almost none of them are telling you the whole story.

Ask about integration in a sales meeting and you'll get a yes. There is an API. There is a certified connector. There may even be a logo slide with your other systems on it. All of that is real. What it describes is a connection — the pipe existing and data moving through it. The pipe is the cheap part.

The expensive part arrives about six weeks after go-live, when someone notices the revenue figure in the reporting layer doesn't match the figure in the PMS, and both are defensible.

Where the cost actually lives

Two systems can be perfectly connected and still disagree, because they were never asked to agree on the questions underneath the data:

  • When does a night belong to a date? Systems disagree about the boundary, and the disagreement only shows up at month end.
  • Is that figure gross or net? Tax handling is the single most reliable source of a two-percent variance nobody can explain.
  • What counts as revenue? Cancellations, no-shows, deposits, credits and adjustments each get treated differently by each platform.
  • What happens to a modified booking? One system amends a record in place; the other writes a new one and leaves the old.
  • Who wins a conflict? When both sides have changed the same record, someone has to lose, and the connector rarely tells you who.

None of these are bugs. They're modelling decisions, made independently by different vendors solving different problems, and the connector's job was never to reconcile them. It was to move the data.

An integration doesn't fail loudly. It drifts quietly, and you find out from a number that looks slightly wrong to someone who knows the business well enough to notice.

The tax is ongoing, not upfront

This is what gets underestimated in the business case. The build has a cost and an end date. The tax has neither. Every connected system adds a permanent obligation: something to monitor, something to reconcile, something that breaks when the vendor ships an update you didn't know about.

Two systems is one relationship. Six systems is fifteen possible relationships, and while you won't connect all of them, you will end up owning more than you planned. That's the curve that surprises people — not the first integration, but the fifth.

What actually helps

I've stopped asking vendors whether they integrate, because the answer is always yes. Better questions:

  • What's your definition of the fields I care about? Get it in writing, before contract.
  • What happens when the connection drops for four hours? Does it backfill, or is that day quietly gone?
  • How do I know it broke? If the answer is "you'll notice", it will be a month before you do.
  • Can I read the data myself? Direct access to the underlying data beats a report you're not allowed to question.

And then build the reconciliation anyway. A small, boring, automated check that compares the two sides daily and shouts when they diverge is worth more than any amount of vendor assurance. It's not glamorous work. It's the difference between finding a variance in a day and finding it in a quarter.

The integration tax isn't avoidable. It's the cost of running a stack instead of a single product, and running a stack is usually the right call. But it should be on the invoice — in the business case, in the resourcing, and in whoever's job description ends up owning it.
