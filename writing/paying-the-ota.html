Distribution

You cannot pay an OTA to leave you alone

The commission was never the price of admission. It was the opening position.

Every OTA programme is voluntary. So was handing over your lunch money. The mistake in both cases is thinking the payment buys something durable, when what it actually buys is the next conversation — the one where there's a new programme, a new tier, and a new explanation for why your listing is underperforming without it.

I've watched a lot of properties work through this, and the pattern is always the same. The commission gets described as high but manageable. Then something is added to it, for good reasons, and the new number becomes the baseline against which the next thing is sold.

Everything is optional

Look at what a large OTA offers a property, and notice that every item is opt-in:

  • Programme discounts. Genius and its equivalents. You fund the discount off your own rate; the platform markets the resulting demand as something it generated.
  • Preferred tiers. Pay a higher commission percentage in exchange for better placement and a badge.
  • Visibility bidding. Bid additional commission points, on specific dates, to move up the list.
  • Sponsored placement. Buy your way above the organic results entirely, on a cost-per-click basis.
  • Rate parity and price matching. Commit to matching, and accept that your rate is now partly set elsewhere.
  • Member, mobile and campaign rates. Another slice off the rate, each with its own justification.

Every one of those is a choice. Every one of them also has a price for declining, paid in rank rather than in dollars, which is exactly why the invoice never shows it. A decision that costs you either way isn't really a choice. It's a menu.

Why clearing the hoops never ends it

Here's the part that gets missed, and it isn't cynicism — it's arithmetic. Placement is finite. There is one top of the list, one first page, one set of results a guest actually reads. If a programme genuinely lifted every property that adopted it, it would stop working the moment everyone adopted it.

So the lift is temporary by construction. Early adopters gain, everyone else follows to stop losing, and once participation is general the advantage is gone — but the cost isn't. You are now paying more to occupy the position you already had.

A programme everyone has adopted is not an advantage. It's the new floor, and the floor is what the next programme gets sold against.

That's the ratchet. Each hoop you clear stops being an achievement and becomes the expectation, and a new tier appears above it, priced accordingly. There is no configuration of your listing that ends this process, because the party setting the hoops is the party collecting.

They own the scoreboard

The deeper problem isn't the price. It's that you're optimising against a metric your counterparty defines, weights, and revises whenever it suits them.

Take a property score. You can work it patiently — content, availability, response times, cancellation handling — and still find the last few points out of reach unless you switch on price matching. Read that plainly: full marks are reserved for properties that have handed over control of their own rate. The score isn't measuring how good you are. It's measuring how compliant you are, and it's marked by the examiner.

Or take sponsored listings. Whatever you did to earn your organic position — rate, reviews, photography, years of consistent operation — a competitor can appear above you by paying. The organic result you optimised for is structurally second place, and the fix on offer is to buy the slot back.

Then the criteria change. Optimise hard against this quarter's scorecard and you've built your operation around a specification you don't own and can't version-control.

What it actually costs

Commission is the number everyone quotes and the smallest part of the answer. By the time a booking lands, the stack usually includes the discount taken off the rate before commission is even calculated, the preferred-tier uplift, the sponsored spend that's gone whether or not the booking survives, and the card fee.

That last one is worth isolating, because it hides well. OTA bookings are largely settled by virtual card, and those cards are issued on foreign BINs as commercial cards — so they reach your acquirer as cross-border commercial transactions, at several times what a domestic guest paying on their own card would cost you. It never appears in a commission comparison because it isn't commission.

Add the cancellation rate, and note which costs survive it: the commission is refunded, the sponsored click that won the booking is not.

I built a calculator for this because the arithmetic is tedious and the answer is usually worse than people expect. On the illustrative defaults it ships with, a channel advertising 15% commission works out at a true cost of sale of about 42% — worse than a flat 18% channel on the same booking, once the programme discount, the uplift, the ads, the card fee and the cancellations are counted. Put your own figures in; the point isn't the number, it's the gap between it and the number you'd have quoted from memory.

The part that isn't their fault

None of this makes an OTA a bad channel, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who says it does. They reach guests you will never reach, in markets you don't advertise in, in languages you don't operate in. They fill inventory you'd otherwise discount to nobody. For a property without a brand, they are the brand. That's a real service and it's reasonable that it costs real money.

The problem is the word partnership. Partnerships don't usually involve one side setting the price, the placement, the grading criteria and the terms on which the criteria change. This is a paid acquisition channel with a sophisticated pricing team, and it behaves exactly like one.

And direct isn't free either. It has a booking engine fee, metasearch and search spend, card costs and a website somebody has to maintain. Any argument that finishes with "just go direct" has skipped the arithmetic. Direct is usually cheaper — the calculator will show you by how much — but cheaper is not the same as free, and a direct booking you bought at any price isn't automatically a win.

Price it, don't fight it

What actually works is unglamorous:

  • Know your cost of sale per channel, all in. Not the commission — the whole stack, including the rate you discounted and the ads you bought. If you can't state it per channel, you're negotiating blind.
  • Treat OTA spend as an acquisition budget. Give it a ceiling and a review date, the way you would any other marketing line. Budgets get questioned annually. Commission rates get questioned approximately never.
  • Decide what each programme is supposed to return. Write it down before you switch it on, then check. "Our visibility improved" is not a result. Incremental room nights at a known cost is a result.
  • Make direct genuinely better, not merely cheaper. A rate you can't beat on their site is table stakes. The thing they can't replicate is everything that happens after the booking.
  • Be honest about dependency. If a channel is 60% of your business, you don't have a negotiating position, you have a landlord. That's a strategic problem, not a commission problem, and it's fixed over years.

You are not going to pay an OTA enough to be left alone. That was never on the menu, and the properties that get furthest are the ones that stop trying — that stop treating each new programme as an imposition to resist or a relationship to protect, and start treating the whole thing as what it is. A channel, with a price, that you either want at that price or don't.

Know the number. Set the ceiling. Then it's just procurement, and procurement you can win.
