Operations

The cost of a checkout

Occupancy tells you how many rooms are full. It tells you nothing about how many changed hands, and changing hands is the work.

Every roster I've ever seen built was built in front of an occupancy forecast. Somebody pulls up the week, reads across the percentages, and puts more people on for the high days. It is such an obvious thing to do that nobody stops to ask whether occupancy is the number that generates the labour.

It isn't. A checkout clean is a different job to a stayover service — the room is stripped, the bathroom gets done properly, the linen is changed, and somebody has to inspect it before it can be sold again. Call it two to three times the work of a service, and that ratio is the whole argument.

Two days that look identical and aren't

Take a hundred rooms occupied on a Monday and a hundred rooms occupied on a Tuesday. On Monday twelve of them are checkouts. On Tuesday forty-four are. Occupancy is flat, the forecast shows a straight line, and one of those days needs half again the labour of the other.

Push it further and it inverts entirely. The busiest night of the week can be the cheapest day to service, because a full house that stays put is mostly stayovers. The quietest night can be the most expensive, because everybody left that morning and every room they left needs turning over.

For fixed room nights, departures are room nights divided by average length of stay. Short stays multiply checkouts at identical occupancy — and checkouts are what you're paying for.

That relationship is the useful part, because it means the labour bill has a driver you can actually influence, and it isn't occupancy.

Where it shows up on the floor

This is not an abstract modelling point. It surfaces as an operational problem, and it usually surfaces wearing somebody else's name.

  • The day the team was flat out at 70%. Rooms were short, the board ran late, someone senior noticed and read it as a performance problem. It was an arithmetic problem, and it was in the roster.
  • The morning after a group departs. Forty rooms leave at once. Occupancy for that night might be modest, and the workload is the largest of the week.
  • Sunday. Weekend leisure stays end, penalty rates apply, and the two compound into the most expensive day of the week on some of the lowest occupancy.
  • The rounding. You cannot roster three tenths of a person. Required hours become whole shifts, and the difference is paid for every week whether or not there is work in it.

In each case the roster was built off a forecast that was accurate about the wrong thing.

Length of stay is a cost lever

Average length of stay gets argued as a revenue metric — better ADR retention, less distribution cost per stay, more on-property spend. All true, and all only half the case.

The other half is that a longer stay is one fewer departure clean. For the same room nights sold, raising average stay trades an expensive checkout for a handful of cheap services. That is a direct labour saving, it recurs every week, and I have never once seen it appear in the business case for a minimum-stay restriction or a stay-longer package. Those get argued on rate integrity, and then the cost side is left on the table.

The caveat, which matters

It is not universally true, and any version of this argument that doesn't say so is selling something.

Longer stays only save money while a departure clean costs more than the servicing of the nights it makes way for. Service every stayover daily and thoroughly, run a quick turnover because your rooms are small and your linen is simple, and the sums invert: the nights you added cost more than the checkout you avoided. A serviced apartment with a light turn and a full daily service can genuinely sit the wrong side of that line.

The line itself is easy enough to find. Compare the minutes in a departure clean against the minutes of stayover servicing that replace it, adjusted for how often you service and how many guests decline. Whichever is larger tells you which way your lever runs.

Occupancy is not a useless number

It drives revenue, linen and consumables, front desk load, breakfast covers, and most of what you'd want to know about how the business is trading. It is a perfectly good number. It is simply the wrong input to one specific decision, and it happens to be the decision that sets your largest controllable cost.

What to do about it

  • Forecast departures, not just occupancy. Your PMS knows tomorrow's departures with far more certainty than it knows tomorrow's occupancy. It is the more reliable number and the more useful one, and almost nobody puts it on the roster sheet.
  • Know your minutes. Time a departure clean and a stayover service, by unit type, properly. Most properties are working off a number somebody estimated years ago and nobody has re-timed since the linen changed.
  • Price the servicing policy. Daily, alternate-day and on-request are three different labour bills. Opt-out programmes get sold as sustainability; they are also a roster decision with a number attached.
  • Look at what the rounding costs. Add up the gap between hours needed and hours rostered across a month. It is usually larger than anyone expects, and it is the cheapest thing on this list to fix.
  • Put the labour saving in the length-of-stay business case. If you're arguing for a two-night minimum, argue the checkout you avoid as well as the rate you protect.

I built a calculator for this because the arithmetic is tedious and the pattern is hard to see from a roster sheet. On the illustrative week it ships with, the busiest night — 110 of 120 units occupied — costs about $8 a room to service, and the quietest night of the same week costs nearly $39. Same property, same team, same rates. The difference is entirely in how many rooms changed hands.

None of this makes the roster easy. Departures are lumpy, people call in sick, and a forecast is still a forecast. But there is a difference between a roster that is wrong because the week moved and a roster that is wrong because it was built on a number that was never measuring the work. The first is unavoidable. The second is just a habit.
