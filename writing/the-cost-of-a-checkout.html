Operations

The cost of a checkout

Late checkout is the only thing on the property people give away without ever pricing. It is usually free to grant and occasionally very expensive, and almost nobody knows which day they're in.

The reason is that housekeeping cost doesn't behave the way it's talked about. It gets described per room — eighteen dollars a clean, say — as though every additional room turned costs eighteen dollars. It doesn't. Once the roster is published, those hours are bought. The forty-first clean of the day costs nothing, and so does the forty-second, right up until the moment somebody has to stay back or come in, and then the next one costs an entire shift.

Housekeeping is a step function, not a slope. The average cost per clean is a number that is wrong in both directions at once — too high inside the roster, far too low outside it.

What's actually fixed

More than people expect, and the split matters because it's the difference between a decision being free and being expensive.

  • The rostered shifts. Committed days in advance, paid whether or not the week turns out as forecast. This is the bulk of it.
  • Public areas, linen and laundry. Broadly the same volume regardless of how many rooms change hands.
  • The rounding. You cannot roster three tenths of a person. Required hours become whole shifts, and the gap is paid for every week.

What's genuinely variable is narrower: the marginal clean, and only when it doesn't fit in hours you've already bought. That's a much smaller category than the per-room framing suggests, and it changes what you should say yes to.

Which brings us to late checkout

A late checkout does not create extra work. It's the same clean, pushed into a narrower window. Total minutes are unchanged — what changes is how much of the shift is left to do them in.

So the cost is nothing while the roster can still absorb it, and real overtime the moment it can't. And the point where that flips moves every single day, with the departure count and the staffing. On a quiet Tuesday with a light board you might absorb a dozen without noticing. On a day where the normal work already runs past two o'clock there is nothing left to give, and every yes is paid at time and a half.

Both of those days get the same answer at most properties, because the answer was decided by whoever was on the desk and how politely the guest asked.

The other half: they're still on the property

A guest who leaves at ten is gone before they can spend anything. A guest who leaves at two has lunch. Maybe a drink by the pool, maybe something from the shop, maybe the kids do the thing you charge for on the way out.

That capture is the actual argument for late checkout, and it's the half that never gets counted because it lands in a different department's revenue line from the cost it creates. Housekeeping wears the labour, food and beverage takes the spend, and nobody nets the two together.

Do net them, and late checkout stops being a goodwill gesture and becomes what it is: a product with near-zero marginal cost and a positive attach rate. Which is a good product.

So price it like one

Not necessarily by charging for it — although you can, and the moment you do the economics change again. Price it in the sense of knowing what it costs on a given day and deciding accordingly.

  • Free, freely, on days with capacity. If the roster absorbs it, granting it costs nothing and returns whatever they spend. Offering it deliberately beats waiting to be asked.
  • Charged on days without. If it runs into overtime, the spend has to beat the overtime, and often it doesn't. A price fixes that — and a guest who'll pay $35 for a 2pm checkout has told you something useful about how much they wanted it.
  • Withheld when the room is needed. On a sold-out night the binding constraint isn't labour at all, it's the arriving guest. Different calculation, and the answer is usually no.
  • As a rate fence. Guaranteed late checkout is a perfectly good thing to attach to a direct booking or a member rate. It costs you nothing on most days and it is worth something to the guest on all of them.

Which is to say: yield it. It's inventory, it has a cost that varies by day, and it has a buyer. That's the whole definition.

The bit I'd push back on

There's an obvious objection: this is a lot of arithmetic for a decision the duty manager makes in four seconds while the guest is standing there.

That's fair, and the answer isn't to compute it live. It's to work it out once for a typical week, find the days that have room and the days that don't, and turn that into a rule simple enough to hand over. Free before eleven any day. Two o'clock free Monday to Thursday. Weekends charged, subject to availability. Never on a sell-out. That's a policy, and it takes about the same four seconds to apply.

The other honest caveat: goodwill is real and it isn't in any of this. Sometimes you say yes to a furious guest at 2pm on the worst possible Sunday because it's the cheapest apology available. That's a good decision and no model will tell you to make it. The point of knowing the number is to know what the apology cost, not to stop making it.

I built a calculator for this because the arithmetic is annoying and the answer is genuinely not obvious. On the illustrative week it ships with, the roster can absorb eighty-one late checkouts at no labour cost whatsoever, and the property is granting forty-eight of them. The other thirty-three are free money nobody is picking up — and on the two days that don't have room, each additional one costs more than the guest is likely to spend.

Both halves of that are worth knowing, and neither is visible from a roster sheet or a P&L. The cost of a checkout isn't a number. It's a different number every day, and the difference is where the decision lives.
