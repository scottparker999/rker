Your BI is only as good as your front desk

Reporting doesn't create truth. It magnifies whatever was typed in at three o'clock on a Saturday by someone with a queue behind them.

There's a particular kind of project that goes like this. The reporting is unreliable, so the business invests in better reporting. New warehouse, new semantic layer, new dashboards, genuinely good work. Six months later the numbers are prettier, faster and still wrong, and now they're wrong with more authority.

Nothing was mis-built. The problem was never in the reporting layer. It was at the point of capture, and no amount of downstream engineering fixes an upstream blank.

What bad capture actually looks like

It's rarely dramatic. It's small, rational shortcuts taken by people under time pressure:

  • The email field filled with a placeholder, because the booking won't save without one.
  • Every walk-in coded to the same generic source, because it's first in the dropdown.
  • A guest who's stayed eleven times existing as eleven separate profiles.
  • Group bookings entered under one name, so the pax count is right and everything else isn't.
  • The notes field doing the work of six proper fields, because it's the only one that never complains.

Every one of those is a sensible decision for the person making it. They're being measured on the queue, not on the data warehouse. The system asked for something that didn't help them do their job, so they gave it the cheapest possible answer.

If a field is optional, it is empty. If a field is mandatory but useless, it is wrong. Those are the only two states.

Treat it as an operations problem

The instinct is to fix this with training, and training helps for about a fortnight. What lasts is changing the economics of the shortcut.

Make the right answer the fast answer. If accurate source coding takes four clicks and the lazy option takes one, you've already chosen the outcome. Reorder the dropdown. Default the common case. Remove the fields nobody uses so the ones that matter feel less like a toll gate.

Validate at entry, not at report. A rule that catches a malformed email while the guest is still standing there is worth more than a monthly exception report nobody's accountable for.

Measure the capture, not just the outcome. This is the part most operators skip. If nobody is scored on data completeness, it competes with everything that is scored — and loses. Put a number on it. Make it visible per site, per shift, per person if you have to. A simple composite score, published and compared, changes behaviour faster than any policy document.

Close the loop. Tell the front desk what the data was for. "Marketing needs emails" is abstract. "We sent an offer to eight hundred past guests and forty of them came back, and that's why the field matters" is not. People will fill in a field they believe in.

The uncomfortable bit

Improving capture makes your numbers look worse before they look better. Suddenly the guest database has fewer contactable records, because the fake ones got cleaned out. The subscriber rate drops because it's finally honest.

That's the moment the project either survives or quietly dies. It helps enormously to warn everybody in advance that the dip is coming, what it means, and roughly how big it'll be. A metric that gets worse for the right reason needs a story attached, or someone will decide the old way was better.

Good reporting is worth building. Just build it knowing that it's a mirror, not a filter, and a mirror is only as useful as whatever you point it at.
